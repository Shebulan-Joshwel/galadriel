Why it matters

Nearby stars, cold brown dwarfs and maybe unknown planets give themselves away by moving across the sky. SPHEREx photographs the whole sky every six months, so that motion shows up when you compare its pictures.

The object you searched for. Position today is where it is now: fast stars drift, so old catalogue positions can be minutes of arc off. Catalogue motion is how fast it crosses the sky in arcseconds per year (″/yr). For scale, the full Moon is about 1,800″ wide.

Detections

Spots that changed between visits, brightest change first. Green: a star Gaia already knows, either moving or flickering. Orange: nothing in Gaia there. It might be noise, an asteroid or a cold object Gaia can't see; measure it to find out. The numbers match the marks on the image.

    Display

    Colours: Deep space is white stars on black; Photographic plate is black on white, like the glass plates Pluto was found on. Stretch and Contrast change how faint things are shown. They don't change the data.

    Overlays

    Drawings on top of the image. Gaia stars: green circles mark stars from ESA's Gaia catalogue, moved to each visit's date; a dashed track means a fast mover. Marks: numbered detections. Artefact flags: areas where the camera recorded glitches (ghosts, streaks). Reticle: target marker, 1′ scale bar.

    Visit grouping

    SPHEREx takes many pictures each time it passes over a spot. They are grouped into visits: a new visit starts after a gap longer than the first number. Long stretches of continuous coverage are split into chunks. Leave these alone unless you know you need them.

    Does it work?

    Before trusting a new discovery, a tool has to get the known answers right. Galadriel was run on real SPHEREx pictures of all five challenge targets and its results were compared with the speeds measured by ESA's Gaia and other catalogues. It measures speed from SPHEREx alone, without looking at the catalogue first.

    How to read it. One SPHEREx pixel is 6.2″ wide, about the width of a coin seen from 800 metres. An error under one pixel means Galadriel puts the object where the catalogue does, to better than the camera's own sharpness. Very bright stars overload the camera's centre, so for them Galadriel measures from the glow around the star and reports anything under 5″ a year as "not moving".

    About the data

    1. Find

      Galadriel asks NASA's Infrared Science Archive for every SPHEREx picture of your target and cuts out just the patch you asked for.

    2. Clean

      Glow from interplanetary dust is removed, glitches are rejected, overloaded star centres are filled, and the pictures are aligned and grouped into visits.

    3. Compare

      Visits are blinked and subtracted. Gaia stars are moved to each visit's date, so a mover without a Gaia match stands out.

    4. Measure

      For any object you click: its speed from SPHEREx alone, its spectrum across 102 infrared colours, and its brightness over time.
